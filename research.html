Precision pulsar timing

Millisecond pulsars spin hundreds of times a second with a regularity that rivals atomic clocks. By predicting the arrival time of every pulse to within tens of nanoseconds over years, we can measure the pulsar's spin, orbit and position — and anything else that perturbs the signal on its way to Earth.

I led the first data releases of the MeerKAT Pulsar Timing Array (MPTA), which times 88 southern millisecond pulsars roughly every two weeks with the MeerKAT radio telescope in South Africa. A large part of this work is noise modelling: separating intrinsic spin noise, dispersion and scattering in the interstellar medium, the solar wind, and instrumental effects from the signals we are looking for. I also contribute to noise modelling for the NANOGrav and Parkes Pulsar Timing Array data sets.

Key papers: MPTA first data release · MPTA 4.5-yr data release · Transdimensional noise modelling · NANOGrav chromatic noise · Scattering variations

Pulsar emission

The best-timed pulsars are not perfectly steady: their pulse shapes jitter from one rotation to the next, switch between emission modes, and vary with observing frequency. These changes limit timing precision, but they are also a window into how and where pulsars emit radio waves.

I discovered mode changing in PSR J1909−3744, the most precisely timed pulsar known, and work on measuring and modelling profile variability, pulse jitter, flux-density variations and glitches across the MeerKAT millisecond pulsar population — and on timing methods that account for them.

Key papers: Mode changing in J1909−3744 · MeerTime PTA emission census · Chromatic jitter in J0437−4715 · Dynamic pulse fitting · Flux densities of 89 MSPs

Nanohertz gravitational waves

Gravitational waves with periods of years — most likely from supermassive black-hole binaries in the centres of merging galaxies — stretch and squeeze the space between Earth and each pulsar. A pulsar timing array looks for the correlated pattern this imprints on the arrival times of pulses from many pulsars across the sky.

I led the first search for gravitational waves with MeerKAT, which found a common signal consistent with the gravitational-wave background in only 4.5 years of data. I also work on mapping the gravitational-wave sky and its anisotropy, searching for individual supermassive black-hole binaries, and combining data sets through the International Pulsar Timing Array.

Key papers: First MeerKAT GW search · MPTA gravitational-wave sky maps · NANOGrav limits on resolvable binaries · IPTA comparison of PTA results

Software & fast inference

Pulsar timing arrays now hold millions of arrival times, and analysing them is increasingly limited by compute. I develop JUG, a JAX-based, GPU-capable pulsar timing package that runs the whole pipeline — clock corrections, timing models, noise and deterministic signals — more than 50 times faster than PINT while agreeing with it at the picosecond level. I also build GPU-accelerated Bayesian noise and signal analyses.

Key papers: JUG: JAX-based Unified pulsar timinG · Code on GitHub

Designing future arrays

Telescope time is the scarcest resource in pulsar timing. I work on how best to spend it — which pulsars to observe, how often and for how long — for MeerKAT today and for the SKA Observatory pulsar timing array to come.

Key papers: A simple optimisation for the MPTA · Towards precision timing with SKA-Mid · The SKAO Pulsar Timing Array
